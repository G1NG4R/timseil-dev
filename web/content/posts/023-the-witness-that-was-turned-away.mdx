## The first red cell

The operations grid on this site had been green or grey since the day it started
measuring. This morning it had a red cell, and the cell was today:

```
{"d":"2026-09-05","state":"outage","downSec":16348}
```

Four hours, thirty-two minutes, twenty-eight seconds. The ninety-one day
availability on the case study went with it, from a hundred per cent to
**77.37 %** — which is `188 / 243`, and both of those numbers can be recounted,
which is the only reason the rest of this post exists.

The site was up the whole time.

## What the record said

The prober runs on GitHub, not here, because a host cannot witness its own
outage. When it finds the site gone it cannot report that to an endpoint on the
missing machine, so it writes a line to a file on a branch instead, and the api
replays the file when it is back. That file had never existed. It is created on
the first outage and not before, and this morning at 01:10 it was created:

```
# uptime-log.txt — machine written by tools/probe.sh (F4).
# Grammar: docs/adr/0038. Two lines per outage, tab separated, UTC.
2026-09-05T01:10:23Z	down	http 4xx
2026-09-05T05:42:51Z	up
```

Two lines, thirteen days after the first measurement, and everything on the
public page follows from them. The replay expands a `down`/`up` pair into the
instants a check would have run at, one every five minutes: **55 instants**, all
failed, from 01:10:23 up to but not including the recovery. Fifty-five failed
checks is well past the two that separate amber from red, so the cell is red.
Fifty-four gaps of three hundred seconds, plus the hundred and forty-eight
seconds from the last replayed instant to the first live check that answered,
is 16 348 seconds, so the cell says four and a half hours. And 55 replayed
failures against 188 live successes — the prober has run 190 times since
23 August, 188 of them clean — is 77.37 %.

Every one of those numbers is arithmetic on two lines of a file. There is no
third source. Which makes the whole public claim rest on one word: `down`.

## Ruling out the obvious

The last deploy had finished at 00:02:30, an hour before. That was the first
suspect and it was wrong twice over: the containers had not moved since.

```
timseildev-…-web-1   ghcr.io/g1ng4r/timseil-web:sha-f828d57   Up 18 hours (healthy)
timseildev-…-api-1   ghcr.io/g1ng4r/timseil-api:sha-f828d57   Up 18 hours (healthy)
```

Eighteen hours, healthy, no restarts. At 01:10 both had been running for over an
hour and both were passing their health checks. Whatever answered the prober,
it was not this application failing.

## The measurement that settled it

The proxy in front of everything keeps two counters that look almost identical
and are not. One counts what a **router** matched. The other counts what a
**service** actually served. A request that is matched and then rejected by a
middleware appears in the first and never in the second, and that gap is a
fingerprint.

The raw counter, scrape by scrape, fifteen seconds apart:

```
traefik_router_requests_total{router="timseil-web@docker", code="403"}
  01:10:15Z   3499
  01:10:30Z   3500      ← the prober's request
```

And the service counter for the same minute: nothing. No request, no response,
no byte. The container's own log agrees — between 01:08 and 01:13 the web
container logged not one line, on a day it logs every request it gets.

So the request reached the front door, was answered **403**, and never got past
it. Not a 404 from a router that had gone missing, not a 500 from the
application, not a timeout. A refusal, by something standing in front, before
anything of mine was consulted.

There is only one thing standing there. The proxy carries an address-reputation
middleware on its HTTPS entrypoint, ahead of every route it serves, and a
rejected address gets a `403`. The other two middlewares on this router can emit
a redirect and a retry, and neither can emit this.

Two more readings finish the picture. In the same minute, other routes behind the
same middleware answered `200` — so it was not failing closed and refusing
everyone, it was making a decision about one address. And nothing in its own
record of local decisions mentions that address, or that morning at all.

That combination says the address was not rejected by anything that happened
here. It was rejected on the strength of a reputation list the middleware did not
author — which leaves no local trace, and is why the refusal is invisible in the
one place I went looking first.

The background rate is the last piece. Over twenty-four hours this site's router
recorded 9 067 responses of `200` and **531 of `403`**. The door has been turning
callers away all along, and every single one of those had been a scanner or a
stranger until this morning, when it was my own witness.

## What was actually wrong

Two things, and only the second is interesting.

**The prober cannot tell a refusal from an absence.** Its vocabulary maps every
non-200 into a reason and every reason into `down`. That is deliberate and it is
written down: the reason is mapped and never quoted, because a curl error carries
the address it could not reach and the log is public. But the mapping flattens a
distinction the same script already makes elsewhere. When the *report* endpoint
answers `401` or `400`, `probe.sh` stops and says so out loud:

> a typo in a secret must not colour a public grid red

That is exactly the right instinct, and it was never applied to the front door.
A `403` at the entrance is not a statement about the host. It is a statement
about the *client* — about my prober, from an address someone else's list does
not like. The building was open. The witness was turned away, and then filed a
report saying the building was gone.

**And a single observation was replayed as a continuous outage.** This is the
larger half. The log's grammar is two lines per outage, a `down` and the `up`
that closes it, and everything between them is reconstructed. That is sound when
the prober runs every five minutes, because then the gap between two lines really
is close to the outage. It runs every five minutes on paper. In practice it ran
six times today. GitHub's scheduler does not owe anyone a cron expression, and
the real spacing between my last two probes was four and a half hours.

So the duration on a public page is not a measurement of the site. It is a
measurement of **how long my prober was asleep**, wearing an outage's clothes. One
refused request became 55 failed checks became four and a half hours became
twenty-three points of availability. Nothing multiplied anything — that was fixed
earlier, and the fix held. The replay is what turned one instant into a span, and
it did so faithfully, from a grammar that assumes a cadence that does not happen.

## What I am not doing

I am not editing the file. The record says what the prober saw, and what it saw
was a `403` it had no better word for. Deleting the line would make the grid
prettier and the log a lie, and the entire point of the log is that it does not
die and does not get tidied.

There is no notch on the grid either. A notch needs a cause, a fix and a
post-mortem, and until the second of those exists this is a post and not a
verdict. The cause is now known to the second. The fix is a decision I would
rather make slowly, because the obvious version — treat `403` as inconclusive
and write nothing — is one careless line away from a prober that cannot report a
real outage the day the door is genuinely shut in everyone's face.

The honest state of this system is: the availability figure on my case study is
currently 77 %, it is derived correctly from what was observed, and what was
observed is wrong. Both of those sentences are true at the same time, and that
is a worse feeling than a red cell.
