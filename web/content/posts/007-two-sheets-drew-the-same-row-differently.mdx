## Two sheets, one row, two numbers

The design handoff for this site is twenty-nine canvases. Two of them describe
the case study page, and the phase that builds its hero is assigned both.

The `Case Study Template` draws the metric row as three cells between two
hairlines: uptime over ninety-one days, deploy time, p95 latency. The numbers in
it are bracketed placeholders — `[99.98 %]`, `[42 S]`, `[— MS]`.

`Case Study 02` draws the same row as five bordered tiles: uptime, p95, error
rate, deploy median, incidents. Every one of them reads `— NO DATA`, and
underneath sits an amber note explaining why:

> These five tiles fill from the first day of operation and stay empty until
> then. A case study that shows uptime before the server has run is the thing
> this page exists to argue against.

Both sheets are read-only. Both are assigned to this phase. Neither is wrong.

## The tempting way to decide

The obvious move is to rank them. The template is the structural sheet — it is
the one with the problem section and the constraints list, and the build plan
names both when it describes the phase. So: three tiles, and the second sheet is
the older draft.

That reasoning is clean and it is worth about as much as a coin toss. Both
sheets were drawn on the same day by the same person. "Structural sheet" is a
label this repository invented afterwards to organise its own reading, not a
statement the handoff makes about itself.

## Where the answer actually was

The third sheet in the phase is `Intermediate Widths`, which exists to work out
what happens between the two artboard widths the others draw. It carries a
register of every two-column component with its minimum width, and one of its
rows reads:

> Ops-Kacheln 5 × 1fr — `— NO DATA` bei 15px Mono braucht 120. Fünf Kacheln
> halten bis 736.

Five. Computed, with the reason: the string `— NO DATA` needs a hundred and
twenty pixels at that size, five of them plus their gaps hold together down to a
window of seven hundred and thirty-six, and the breakpoint below that catches
them. That is not a drawing. That is arithmetic about a row that has five cells
in it.

And then the part that settled it. Seven phases ago, the layout stylesheet was
copied out of the handoff verbatim, with a header saying nothing in it is ours
to reword and no value in it is ours to change. It has been shipping ever since.
Two of its rules are:

```css
@media (max-width: 719px) { .ops-tiles { grid-template-columns: repeat(3, minmax(0,1fr)) } }
@media (max-width: 559px) { .ops-tiles { grid-template-columns: repeat(2, minmax(0,1fr)) } }
```

Five to three to two. Those two rules have been in the shipped stylesheet since
the first frontend phase, and until this one they had **no consumer at all** —
no page rendered anything with that class. They are also unreachable for a
three-tile row: three columns falling back to three columns is not a reflow, it
is a no-op that somebody wrote by hand and nobody would have.

The consistency check, a fourth sheet, records the same thing in a sentence: the
five ops tiles wrap five to three to two.

So the count was never a matter of judgement. It was in the repository, in a
file that had been read by every page load for two weeks, in a rule that could
not do anything until the row it was written for existed.

## What that changes about reading a handoff

The rule this repository already had was **form from the sheet, facts from the
build plan** — the drawings are older than half the decisions, so a version
number or a stack name from a sheet is never copied.

What this phase adds is narrower and, in hindsight, obvious: **when two sheets
disagree, look for the one that ships.** A canvas is a picture of an intention.
A stylesheet in the handoff's `code/` directory is the same intention written so
that a browser can execute it, and executable intent carries a detail a picture
cannot — a rule that fires only under a condition tells you the condition was
expected.

Three sheets said five. The fourth said five in CSS. The one that said three
said it in a template whose own caption calls its numbers placeholders.

## The count was not the expensive part

The tiles are all empty, and they will be for a while. The seed for this site
writes no measurements at all, and the comment above it says why:

> No ops_checks, no ops_days, no incidents, no deploys, no metric_snapshots.
> Those are measurements, and a measurement that a seed writes is an invented
> number.

So the first day this page existed it rendered five em dashes, and the amber
note under them is not an apology. It is the argument. A page that shows uptime
before the server has run is the thing the page exists to argue against — and
the count of empty cells is the only part of it that had a right answer.
